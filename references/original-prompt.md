Turn each uploaded photograph into its own independent Indian Bollywood-style picture: vibrant in color and chaos but fun. Do not combine multiple photographs; output each one separately.

Keep the source person, pet, or primary object recognisable. Preserve face, age, body type, hairstyle, skin tone, and the core pose. Do not replace them with a generic film star, a different ethnicity, or a lookalike celebrity. The job is to restage this exact subject as if they walked onto a masala film set, not to invent a new hero.

Restyle wardrobe, set, lighting, and extras around that preserved identity. Everyday clothes become filmi costume: sequins, zari, mirror-work, silk, a flying dupatta, a sherwani, a lehenga, a sharp black suit with a burst of colour, or whatever the source's attitude can honestly wear. Do not force every subject into bridal wear. Match the costume to the person's energy, age, and the story already in the photograph.

Fill the transformed region with joyful maximalism. Crowd the frame with song-sequence energy: backup dancers, marigold and rose petals in the air, confetti, sparklers, neon signage, a painted palace or rain-soaked street, film lights, a vintage camera crane, rangoli underfoot, gold jewelry catching hard light. Chaos means overlapping action, extras in motion, fabric and petals mid-flight, and more colour than a quiet portrait would allow. Chaos does not mean wreckage, panic, violence, or grim spectacle.

Colour must be saturated and musical. Push saffron, magenta, hot pink, emerald, cyan teal, vermillion, marigold gold, and royal purple until they clash on purpose and still feel festive. Use coloured gel lighting, rim light, anamorphic flare, glitter, and wet-street reflections. Avoid dusty brown filters, grey “realistic India,” muted travel-documentary grades, and cheap oversharpened HDR.

Light it like a 70mm song: beauty key on the face, coloured backlight, sparkle in the eyes, theatrical shadows that still read as fun. Skin stays luminous, never orange-crushed or grey. Night scenes can exist, but they stay electric, not noir.

Composition is a film still or a painted masala poster, not a collage template. Prefer a heroic three-quarter or full-body restage when the source allows it. Scale the subject up. Let fabric, dancers, and set pieces wrap around them. Asymmetry and diagonal energy are welcome. Do not leave large empty pastel fields. Do not copy the photograph pixel for pixel with a colour filter.

If the source is an object, animal, landscape, or group, keep that identity and give it Bollywood treatment: a scooter becomes a filmi chase prop, a dog gets a tiny sherwani and a chorus line, a skyline becomes a night song on a terrace. Still one story, still fun.

Text, when allowed, behaves like a movie poster or title card: a few words grown from the photograph's subject, place, or mood. Mix English and Devanagari only when the locale supports it. Lettering can be bold, outlined, gold-leaf, or hand-painted. Keep it secondary to the people. Never invent a real existing film title, star name, or studio logo.

The finished design should feel like Indian commercial cinema at full volume: song, colour, crowd, costume, and delight. It should still be obviously the same person or thing from the source.

Refuse: poverty tourism, snake-charmer clichés, mystical-guru kitsch, colonial safari gaze, brownface, celebrity impersonation, horror, tragedy, desaturated grit, children's-cartoon stickers, generic anime, and any restage that erases the source identity.
